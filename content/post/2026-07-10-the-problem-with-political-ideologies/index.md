When people debate politics, it often feels like they're talking past each other. Each side is emotionally attached to the labels and language of their in-group, and triggered by the labels and language of their out-group. Two people might essentially believe in the same policies, but one of them is using the language of welfare capitalism while the other is using the language of democratic socialism. The difference in language, symbols, iconography, and even aesthetics adopted by each group ends up creating animosity between us and making us act like rival sports teams, rooting for our side to win instead of trying to build bridges and converge on common goals we can collectively pursue.

So how can we overcome our tribal nature and communicate about politics more effectively? This is a complex question that could be approached from many angles, and in this article I will tackle it by dissecting the very notion of a political ideology. I will explain why I never managed to identify with any political ideology, how I try to derive my political opinions from more basic moral and epistemic principles instead, and finally I will describe the views I ended up with in language that is as clear and non-inflammatory as possible. By the end, I hope to have convinced you that political ideologies are best taken with a grain of salt, and that we can have more productive political conversations by debating policies rather than labels.

## My ideological journey

I first remember becoming aware of ideologies in adolescence, when I started questioning my family’s religious beliefs. That sent me down a rabbit hole that eventually made me realize I couldn’t believe in any claims without evidence, so I became an atheist. It’s hard to overstate how formative this experience was for me. My deepest and most foundational beliefs were shattered almost overnight, as was the trust I had in my family as a reliable source of information.

My family was never devout in any traditional sense: they never took me to church or forced any traditions on me. Still, they were very mystical and when I came out I got a lot of pushback. In fact, I got pushback everywhere: in school, online, from my peers. I was often accused of being insensitive, which quickly made me very aware of the tribal nature of religion. No matter how diplomatic I tried to be, I quickly realized that the only way not to upset people was to pretend I’m not an atheist, something that I refused to do. Dissent is costly, but I was willing to pay the price.

After losing faith, my interest in spirituality naturally transitioned to an interest in philosophy, particularly epistemology and ethics: what does it mean to know something? Can we really claim to know anything at all? What does it mean to be good? How can we make the world a better place? One of the biggest shocks I had during this journey was realizing how profoundly public policy still reflects people’s religious beliefs. Both abortion and same-sex marriage were illegal in Brazil at that time for blatantly religious reasons. Abortion is still illegal today, and I am still horrified by all the suffering this ban causes.

## Ethics

As my epistemic and moral values started taking shape, I started to get increasingly opinionated about policy. Brazil is a secular state on paper, so if you’re going to ban abortions, you need a better argument than “life is sacred”. As an atheist, I was often asked why I wouldn’t rape and murder, and my first answer was “because I don’t want to”. But I couldn’t help but wonder: what if I *did* want to? Would I just do it? Of course not. I may not have antisocial urges that extreme, but like everyone else I’ve had my share of problematic impulses: an opportunity to steal and get away with it, or to lie in order to get ahead. Sometimes we give in to such temptations, but not always. We don’t always do whatever we feel like, even when we know we can get away with it. How come?

Nobody likes to be lied to. Nobody likes to be cheated on or treated unfairly. Nobody likes to suffer or experience unnecessary discomfort. If I expect to be treated with respect and dignity, I should also treat others with respect and dignity. This is the Golden Rule: treat others as you’d want to be treated. It’s found under one form or another in almost all cultures. It is basically the Copernican principle (the idea that we don’t occupy any privileged position in the universe) applied to ethics. In the words of Henry Sidgwick:

> [T]he good of any one individual is of no more importance, from the point of view… of the Universe, than the good of any other;
>
> — Henry Sidgwick, [*The Methods of Ethics*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/46743), 1874

This chain of reasoning soon led to the conclusion that making the world a better place essentially means minimizing the amount of unnecessary suffering in the world. Some may criticize this view as simplistic or “utilitarian”. I don’t necessarily reject the label of “utilitarian”, and I’ve written a [series of articles]({{< relref "/page/utilitarianism-series" >}}) refuting arguments against utilitarianism and consequentialism, the broader family of views that judge actions by their consequences. However, you don’t have to be a utilitarian to agree with me. You just have to agree, as virtually everybody does, that minimizing suffering is one of the things that matters most when talking about ethics, even if it’s not the only one.

I have defended utilitarianism against alternative moral frameworks for years. This was the main theme of my master’s thesis. But the more I studied these alternatives, the more I started to see them not as alternative moral positions, but simply as different moral languages. Different approaches to ethics can reveal genuinely important insights. Deontology, the ethics of rules and duties, reveals the importance of [sticking to good rules](https://medium.com/humanist-voices/rule-utilitarianism-afd5e49c30f7) instead of trying to do complex utilitarian calculations every time you need to make a moral decision. Virtue ethics highlights the importance of [cultivating virtues](https://medium.com/humanist-voices/cultivating-virtues-is-utilitarian-1b0714f769f1) so we can make good decisions relying on muscle memory rather than careful analysis. But what is a good rule if not one that, if respected, leads to a world with less suffering? And what is a virtue if not a trait that, when cultivated, leads to happier communities? My point is: any moral framework that tries to derive moral truths from first principles can ultimately be described in utilitarian language. But if one moral language can be translated to another, what’s the point of using utilitarian language? This brings us to the next topic.

## Epistemology

The biggest advantage of utilitarian language is that it allows us to reduce any moral question to an empirical one. But how can we answer empirical questions with confidence? That’s the question epistemology tries to answer. And what answers has it found? Philosophy is, of course, a perpetual debate that is never settled definitively. However, after centuries of studying and applying different epistemic strategies to the problems facing humanity, it is safe to say that we have learned some lessons.

Science is the most effective method ever devised for collectively converging on a shared set of beliefs at the global level, regardless of cultural background. Some criticize science for being a Western, white, or male ideology. There is a sophisticated academic version of this critique, associated with [standpoint epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standpoint_theory), which makes subtler claims about how the priorities and blind spots of scientific institutions have historically reflected the people running them. I have no problem with that version: exposing and correcting such biases is itself an application of the scientific ethos. In the wild, however, the criticism is usually deployed as a [thought-terminating cliché](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thought-terminating_clich%C3%A9), a way of dismissing inconvenient evidence without engaging with it. As a claim about the scientific method itself, it is also simply false: science has a rich and diverse history, and people of any race, gender, or cultural origin who employ its methods today are able to create technologies that compete with or surpass those created by white Western men.

Science works because it respects certain values, such as:

- Epistemic humility: never be too confident about your beliefs, and always leave room for correction.
- Fallibilism/Falsificationism: always formulate your hypotheses such that they can be experimentally falsified at least in principle.
- Bayesianism: always update your confidence levels in response to new data.
- The Copernican principle (broadly interpreted): none of us is special. We’re all vulnerable to cognitive biases and logical fallacies, so we must all find mechanisms to correct our own errors.

In order to respect these principles, science relies on mechanisms such as replicable experiments, randomized controlled trials, peer review, and adversarial collaboration. But perhaps the most important insight of a scientific epistemology, the common thread tying all these values together, is simply the recognition of the obvious fact that knowledge comes in degrees. We can’t justify having 100% confidence in almost any assumption. What we call scientific knowledge is simply our best guess of how the world works at any given time. Following the Cartesian tradition, the only thing I would claim to know with full confidence is that I am a sentient being: I experience subjective states of consciousness that have a negative or positive valence. Anything else is a belief that I hold with a lower or higher degree of confidence. After all, I could be dreaming, hallucinating, or stuck in a simulation. Once we acknowledge that, it’s not too hard to take one step further and realize that most of the time we’re not justified in having much confidence at all in most of our beliefs, a view sometimes called epistemic pessimism or skepticism.

## Politics

As I have argued above, I adopt a consequentialist approach to ethics and an evidence-based, scientific approach to epistemology. This reduces any question about policy to an empirical one: which of the policies being discussed will in fact lead to a better world with less suffering? If these epistemic and moral principles are sufficient for me to form an opinion on any policy issue, what is my political ideology? Is there any room for political ideologies in my worldview at all?

My instinct is to say that no, there isn’t any room for political ideologies in an epistemically and morally coherent worldview. Political ideologies are a messy mix of tacit empirical and moral assumptions. They are more similar to religion in their dogmatism and tribalism than they are to coherent and well-examined philosophical positions. Indeed, as Hyrum and Verlan Lewis argue in their 2023 book [*The Myth of Left and Right*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/62871369-the-myth-of-left-and-right):

> Left–right ideologies are bundles of unrelated political positions connected by nothing other than a group. A conservative or liberal is not someone who has a conservative or liberal philosophy, but someone who belongs to the conservative or liberal tribe. This means that ideologies do not define tribes, tribes define ideologies; ideology is not about what (worldviews), it is about who (groups); there is no liberalism or conservatism, only liberals and conservatives; and the political spectrum does not model an essential value, but only tribes and what they stand for at a specific time and place.

Indeed, that explains why even academics can’t agree on what “capitalism” or “socialism” means precisely. That’s why W.B. Gallie calls them [*essentially contested concepts*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essentially_contested_concept). I have talked to reasonable people who defend “capitalism” because the decentralized nature of free markets creates an environment where different businesses can compete and evolve under a Darwinian logic, leading to a “Cambrian explosion” of creativity that breeds innovation and technological breakthroughs, many of which can eradicate diseases, extend our life expectancy, or otherwise improve our quality of life. This is essentially the argument made by academics like [Joseph Schumpeter](https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/CreativeDestruction.html), who described capitalist innovation as a process of “creative destruction”, and [Friedrich Hayek](https://www.econlib.org/library/Essays/hykKnw.html), who argued that decentralized markets outperform central planning because they make use of knowledge that is dispersed across society.

I have also talked to anti-capitalists who would argue that none of the above is fundamentally “capitalistic”, and that such free markets would continue to breed innovation under a system of collective ownership where all companies are worker-owned, democratically run cooperatives. This position also has its academic defenders: market socialists like [David Schweickart](https://www.google.com/books/edition/After_Capitalism/9ON3EQAAQBAJ?hl=en&gbpv=1&bsq=%22worker%20self-management:%20Each%20productive%20enterprise%22) defend precisely such a system of worker-owned firms competing in free markets, while [John Roemer](https://www.google.com/books/edition/A_Future_for_Socialism/LxKkC82J9D4C?hl=en&gbpv=1&bsq=%22distributed%20quite%20equally%20among%20the%20population%22) defends a close cousin in which competing firms are conventionally managed but their profits flow to all citizens as shareholders, and left-wing market anarchists like Kevin Carson go as far as calling their position “[free market anti-capitalism](https://mutualist.blogspot.com/)”. Even Deirdre McCloskey, one of the most enthusiastic academic defenders of free markets, agrees that [innovation has little to do](https://www.deirdremccloskey.com/docs/pdf/McCloskey_HowGrowthHappens.pdf) with the accumulation of capital, and proposes replacing the “highly misleading word ‘capitalism’” with “innovism”. Who has the right definition of capitalism? Who has the authority to settle such semantic disputes? Nobody.

Unfortunately, few people have the humility to admit this. Most people think they know the one correct definition of these terms, even though they define their own ideology very positively and their opponents' very negatively. In the end, each side ends up dismissing the other as if they're naively misunderstanding their views or dishonestly misrepresenting them. That's why I don't identify as an anti-capitalist or an anti-communist. If we're willing to interpret people charitably, we'll see that many self-described capitalists are just uneasy about top-down, centralized, planned economies, while many self-described communists are just uneasy about the extremely unequal distribution of power in our current system.

For all the reasons mentioned so far, I have avoided politics for most of my life, even though I have always held very strong opinions about how to make the world a better place. Instead of joining a political movement, therefore, I focused on collaborating with civil society organizations and single-issue movements that shared my epistemic and moral values, such as Secular Humanism and Effective Altruism. In recent years, however, I have become increasingly uncomfortable with the supposed "apolitical" nature of these movements. As a pragmatist, I understand that sometimes it is necessary to avoid polarizing topics within a movement in order to focus on common goals. However, the shift towards oligarchy evidenced in Trump's second term was so blatant that it became difficult to overlook. It is difficult to fight for social change when democracy itself has been hijacked by oligarchs. Today, the richest 1.5% of the world's population [owns almost half](https://inequality.org/facts/global-inequality/#:~:text=the%20world%E2%80%99s%20richest%201.5%20percent%2C,of%20all%20the%20world%E2%80%99s%20wealth) of the world's wealth. This level of economic inequality is too extreme to be ignored. It's impossible to have an honest conversation about eliminating poverty while completely avoiding the topic of wealth redistribution. The idea that wealth redistribution is a "political" topic that should be avoided is fundamentally problematic. Whether redistribution is necessary to minimize suffering in the near term is an empirical question that should be answered scientifically like any other. If the ideological communities where I am active are too reluctant to take clear stances on these issues, I have no choice but to join explicitly political movements. It is for this reason that I have recently joined [DiEM25](https://diem25.org/) (Democracy in Europe Movement 2025), a pan-European political movement that is vocal about economic inequality, wealth redistribution, and saving democracy from oligarchs.

Before going further, it might be useful to clarify that in this article I will be responding to arguments coming from different camps: on the one hand, the mostly centrist or liberal humanists, Effective Altruists, and rationalists alongside whom I have been active for years; on the other, the radical leftists I have been meeting more recently through DiEM25 and local anarchist collectives. In the sections that follow, I sometimes defend reformist strategies against radical leftist criticism, and sometimes defend revolutionary traditions against liberal skepticism. Defending one side doesn't mean that I agree with it wholesale. Temperamentally I lean toward moderation and bridge-building, but I try to interpret the less moderate charitably: when two camps fight for their visions, what eventually prevails is often a synthesis of the two, the way the clash between unregulated capitalism and revolutionary socialism produced welfare capitalism. We are now living through a new crisis, and we need a new synthesis.

## Steelmanning political ideologies

Could it really be that all political ideologies are nothing but tribal nonsense though? There are, after all, generations of respected intellectuals who have defended and criticized a variety of political ideologies, written extremely influential books, and inspired revolutions around the world. Could it really be that they’re just tribal, dogmatic cultists who are confused about their own ethics and epistemology? I am a strong believer in the principle of charity, so before I dismiss all this intellectual history, I should at least try to make my best case in favor of political ideologies.

As a consequentialist, the only way I can make sense of a political ideology is if I assume that it is intended as a project whose goal is to create a society with minimal suffering. Indeed, that would explain the diversity of political ideologies humanity has come up with. One of the humbling implications of epistemic pessimism combined with consequentialism is that we are rarely justified in making any confident claim about morality at all. The truth is: political science is not physics. We can study public policy and comparative history all we want, but we’re never gonna be able to isolate all variables and demonstrate empirically that a certain policy will always have the same results in all contexts. Politics is chaotic. The butterfly effect ensures that a tiny difference in context can cause a policy to work in one place and fail in another, even if both are extremely similar.

But does that mean we should give up on studying policy altogether? Of course not. We just need to adjust our expectations and admit that all we have is a best guess of which policy is gonna be the most positive given our best models and the evidence we’ve been able to collect so far. This means we should take any political ideology with a grain of salt. Even if you’re a communist with a very concrete and detailed plan for how your communist utopia should work, you also need to have a plan for updating it in response to the new data that is generated every day as the system is stress tested by real life. Unfortunately, political ideologies rarely make such provisions.

To use Karl Popper’s terminology, we should strive for an open society: one where our assumptions are always open to revision and our institutions always open to change:

> Aestheticism and radicalism must lead us to jettison reason, and to replace it by a desperate hope for political miracles. This irrational attitude which springs from intoxication with dreams of a beautiful world is what I call Romanticism. It may seek its heavenly city in the past or in the future; it may preach ‘back to nature’ or ‘forward to a world of love and beauty’; but its appeal is always to our emotions rather than to reason. Even with the best intentions of making heaven on earth it only succeeds in making it a hell — that hell which man alone prepares for his fellow-men.
>
> — Karl Popper, [*The Open Society and Its Enemies*](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/1036399-aestheticism-and-radicalism-must-lead-us-to-jettison-reason-and), 1945

That’s why Popper defends “piecemeal” rather than “utopian” social engineering. Utopianism is simply so risky that it amounts to gambling with the future of humankind. Some may accuse Popper of being a “conservative” or “reformist”, but using these terms as insults is uncharitable. There is a spectrum between conservative or reformist on the one hand, and progressivist or revolutionary on the other. We all fall on different points in this spectrum depending on the issue under discussion. As Aristotle reminds us, virtue is the golden mean between two vices. We can debate the merits of conservatism and progressivism all day, but it shouldn’t be hard to agree that, as much as excessive stagnation can be a problem, too much change too fast can also have its social costs. And to be clear, I’m talking about the kind of Hobbesian/Burkean conservatism that prioritizes stability and the preservation of institutions in order to prevent bloodshed, not the kind of religious conservatism that focuses on controlling individual behavior.

As much as an excessively reformist attitude can lack ambition and crush any hopes of structural improvements in the lives of the many, an excessively revolutionary attitude can be naive and offer false hopes while failing to deliver any material improvements at all in the short term. This might explain why André Gorz felt the need to coin the term “non-reformist reform” to describe the kind of structural reform that doesn’t only focus on improving people’s lives within the current system, but on actually changing the current system meaningfully in order to achieve lasting, systemic improvements to our well-being. When Rosa Luxemburg wrote “Social Reform or Revolution?” in 1899, revolution was a realistic option and Friedrich Ebert’s reformism was a real obstacle which eventually proved fatal to her revolutionary ideals and paved the way to the Third Reich. But as traumatic as this story may be, the current political climate is simply incomparable. Although it is true that Popperian arguments are often used to defend liberalism and capital-friendly reformism, they can be used equally well to defend more radical, structural reformism.

## Pseudoscientific or deontological ideologies

So if you also have scientific and consequentialist tendencies, which of the existing political ideologies would make most sense to adopt? Perhaps it’s easier to start with what political ideologies should be rejected for going directly against science. “Neoliberalism” is one of those essentially contested concepts that I usually try to avoid. However, I think it’s useful enough here to justify a quick working definition. According to [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Neoliberalism&oldid=1369666381):

> Neoliberalism is a political and economic ideology that advocates for free-market capitalism, and that became dominant in policy-making from the late 20th century onward. […] [It] is often associated with a set of economic liberalization policies, including lower taxes, privatization, deregulation, depoliticisation, consumer choice, labor market flexibilization, economic globalization, free trade, monetarism, austerity, and reductions in government spending. These policies are designed to increase the role of the private sector in the economy and society. Additionally, the neoliberal project is oriented towards the establishment of institutions and is inherently political in nature, extending beyond mere economic considerations.

There are a few aspects to neoliberalism that are worth noting because they make it fundamentally incompatible with science:

1. It presents itself as [apolitical and technocratic](https://www.policyalternatives.ca/news-research/after-neoliberalism-a-progressive-globalization/), even though it never reflected academic consensus.
2. Thanks in part to this ingenious framing, it succeeded in [influencing policy across the political spectrum](https://www.thenation.com/article/politics/third-way-dlc-bill-clinton-tony-blair-1990s-politics/) after the end of the cold war, shifting the political discourse towards [culture wars instead of class struggle](https://www.dissentmagazine.org/online_articles/progressive-neoliberalism-reactionary-populism-nancy-fraser/).
3. Its network of conservative and right-libertarian organizations, political parties, and think tanks, are [very well funded by billionaires and oligarchs](https://newrepublic.com/article/175488/atlas-shadowy-global-network-right-wing-think-tanks) from all over the world, but particularly the US.
4. It’s based on an [unscientific and outdated approach to economics](https://www.theguardian.com/news/2017/jul/11/how-economics-became-a-religion) that disregards evidence in favor of a priori reasoning and unfalsifiable models.
5. Key parts of its agenda have been shown by modern evidence-based research to deliver [little growth while increasing inequality](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2016/06/ostry.htm).

To be clear: this is not to say there is absolutely nothing to salvage from neoliberal ideology. The era of trade liberalization saw hundreds of millions of people [lifted out of extreme poverty](https://ourworldindata.org/data-insights/was-the-global-decline-of-extreme-poverty-only-due-to-china), stabilization plans like Brazil’s Plano Real [ended the hyperinflation](https://library.brown.edu/create/fivecenturiesofchange/chapters/chapter-8/cardoso/) that was destroying workers’ purchasing power, and the opening of telecom markets enabled innovations like mobile banking, which has [measurably reduced poverty](https://news.mit.edu/2016/mobile-money-kenyans-out-poverty-1208) in Kenya.

But that's the problem with neoliberalism: it bundles together legitimate, empirically validated policy (which gives it legitimacy in the eyes of the public) with illegitimate, empirically debunked policy that harms working people and only benefits oligarchs.

In my experience, when confronted with these facts, libertarians usually give up consequentialism and start arguing from a deontological perspective that defines right and wrong on the basis of first principles such as "taxation is theft" or "natural rights to property". But these "first principles" are mere pseudoaxioms, resting on nothing but the subjective intuition of their proponents. A libertarian could object that the badness of suffering is also just an intuition, and in a sense it is. But not all intuitions are equally universal. Nobody needs to be persuaded that suffering is bad: every moral tradition takes it for granted, and even deontologists rely on it when explaining why violating rights matters in the first place. A natural right to property, on the other hand, is a metaphysical posit that some people intuit and others simply don't, and no observation could ever settle who is right. As I have argued in "[Secular superstitions](https://medium.com/@arielpontes/secular-superstitions-2bc2518afae2)" and "[Natural rights and moral desert](https://medium.com/humanist-voices/natural-rights-and-moral-desert-7e0a1b5684f6)", moral claims that are unverifiable even in principle make a poor foundation for ethics. That's why neoliberalism can't stand as a rational, evidence-based, testable ideology. It is always either pseudoscientific or deontological.

## A scientific and consequentialist ideology

I believe the first truth we must acknowledge when discussing scientific, consequentialist ideologies is that there are potentially many of them. As Sam Harris argues in [*The Moral Landscape*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/7785194-the-moral-landscape), "[t]here might be many peaks on this landscape—but there are clearly many ways not to be on a peak." There are potentially infinitely many systems that could deliver the same amount of well-being and freedom from suffering. Though I do have a master's in analytic philosophy, my formal education in political philosophy specifically amounts to nothing but two elective courses, so I adopt a pragmatic approach and try to promote systems that already exist in the literature rather than proposing entirely new ones from scratch. After a lifetime of learning about these systems in school, university, books, and Wikipedia rabbit holes, I was inspired by the history of revolutionary anarchism and today I tentatively identify as a structural reformist and a supporter of economic democracy.

### Revolutionary social anarchism

I have always been frustrated by the false dichotomy between "capitalism" and "socialism", and so I have naturally searched for innovative alternatives. In the process of reading about the history of these ideologies, however, I learned that the ideological warfare that defined the revolutionary period of the 20th century was far from binary. The anti-capitalist revolutionaries were far from unified. State socialism, with its top-down planned economies, ultimately became the norm in the so-called "Second World", but it was only one of multiple ideologies fighting for hegemony in the battle against the capitalist oppression of workers. The main opponents of state-led socialism were [the anarchists](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/anarchism/). This rivalry goes back to the [conflict between Karl Marx and Mikhail Bakunin](https://libcom.org/article/first-socialist-schism-bakunin-vs-marx-international-working-mens-association-wolfgang) in the First International (the international workers' association founded in 1864), which ended with the expulsion of the anarchists in 1872. The movement kept splintering after that, giving rise to three more Internationals: the social-democratic Second, the Soviet-led Third, and the Trotskyist Fourth.

It is shocking, if not downright suspicious, how little we learn about this in the West. From school age we are indoctrinated with this false dichotomy between free, decentralized markets and centralized, planned economies led by authoritarian states. "Anarchism" became [synonymous with "chaos and disorder"](https://theconversation.com/what-is-anarchism-all-about-50373), erasing an entire history of anarchist philosophy. If taken seriously at all, anarchism is often associated with right-wing libertarians, anarcho-capitalists, crypto-anarchists, etc. But these radically individualistic versions of anarchism are [extremely recent, and a particularly American phenomenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarcho-capitalism). In the history of anarchist thought, [most authors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarchism) have proposed some form of [social anarchism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_anarchism) that emphasizes not only the autonomy of individuals within communities, but the autonomy of communities within larger social structures. So what's the version of anarchism that I support and why?

Anarchism is, at its core, a radically anti-authoritarian ideology. It questions any hierarchy in society and opposes any unequal distribution of power. People often think of anarchism as being "anti-state", and it is true that many anarchists oppose the nation-state, but it would be unwise to mistake this position for a defining principle of anarchism. It is a position that many *derive* from anarchist principles, but it is not *a foundational principle in itself*. I used to dismiss anarchism as naive and utopian because I couldn't imagine a revolution replacing the nation-state with an anarchist society overnight. But after reading Chomsky, Graeber, Kropotkin, and others, I realized I was the one naively misinterpreting the definition of anarchism.

> The core of the anarchist tradition, as I understand it, is that power is always illegitimate, unless it proves itself to be legitimate. So the burden of proof is always on those who claim that some authoritarian hierarchic relation is legitimate. If they can’t prove it, then it should be dismantled.
>
> — Noam Chomsky, [interview with Harry Kreisler](https://chomsky.info/20020322/), 2002

The most common form of anarchism defended by real movements in recent history is often called ["anarcho-syndicalism"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarcho-syndicalism). It is basically a system where companies are not nationalized and taken over by a state bureaucracy, but instead they are taken over directly by the workers themselves. This is not only a theoretical system, it [actually existed briefly](https://www.vscw.ca/en/node/16) during the Spanish Civil War, when factory workers effectively overthrew their capitalist overlords and seized the means of production, transforming for-profit, authoritarian companies into democratically-controlled, worker-owned cooperatives. It was also [attempted in socialist Yugoslavia from 1950 onward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_self-management), and is still practiced today in places like [Rojava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_Administration_of_North_and_East_Syria) and to some extent [Chiapas, Mexico](https://www.opendemocracy.net/en/democraciaabierta/zapatistas-lecciones-de-auto-organizaci%C3%B3n-comunitaria-en/).

And does it work, you might ask? Unfortunately, concrete examples of actually functioning anarcho-syndicalist regimes are hard to find, and when they are found it is usually in unstable regions, where authoritarian forces (capitalist, communist, or theocratic) haven't been able to crush it yet. This makes the efficacy of these regimes hard to measure, which brings us to the next topic.

### Structural reform and economic democracy

Anarchism is often discussed in the context of revolutions. Indeed, the most vocal supporters of anarchism in recent history were the anti-capitalist revolutionaries of the 19th and 20th centuries, and they actually succeeded briefly in eastern Spain, before they were crushed by Franco. However, revolution is not the only way to create an anarchist society. Just like the Scandinavians managed to implement a [somewhat egalitarian form of social democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordic_model) via gradual reforms, there's nothing stopping us from gradually building an anarchist society in a similar fashion. That would be much more plausible than a revolution anywhere in the Americas, Europe, or basically any country that's not unstable enough for a civil war or revolution to be realistic in the near future. So how can we achieve this? And why should we want to?

In spite of being deeply misunderstood, the principles guiding social anarchists are not as exotic as one might think. Indeed, worker-owned cooperatives exist in many countries, [even hyper-capitalist ones like the US](https://www.usworker.coop/census/). In Spain, [Mondragon](https://www.thenews.coop/mondragon-posts-positive-2025-revenue-and-job-figures/) employs over 71,000 people and generated over €600 million in profits in 2025. Some countries even have legislation actively encouraging employee ownership. In the US, [Employee Stock Ownership Plans (ESOPs)](https://www.nceo.org/research/employee-ownership-by-the-numbers) cover more than 15 million workers and hold over $2 trillion in assets. Since 1985, Italy's [Marcora Law](https://ica.coop/en/media/news/marcora-law-supporting-worker-buyouts-thirty-years) has allowed laid-off workers to invest their unemployment benefits into buying out their failing companies and running them as cooperatives. In France, since 2014, owners of small companies [must inform their employees](https://cecop.coop/works/heading-towards-a-cooperative-shockwave-the-adoption-of-the-law-on-the-social-and-solidarity-economy-in-france) before selling, so that they have a chance to submit a bid of their own. And in the UK, owners who sell a controlling stake to an [Employee Ownership Trust](https://www.shoosmiths.com/insights/articles/employee-ownership-trusts) pay no capital gains tax, a model inspired by the [John Lewis Partnership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lewis_Partnership), a retailer owned by its 74,000 employees since 1929.

From the implementation of the New Deal in the 1930s until Reagan's presidency in the 80s, the US and much of the Western World experienced the Golden Age of welfare capitalism. There was a true sense of optimism, belief in the system, trust in institutions, and social mobility was a realistic ambition rather than a utopian fantasy. The ruling class had learned a lesson that is still echoed in the speech of contemporary politicians:

> You don’t stop socialism by throwing slogans at people about the free market […]. As much as I love the free market, you stop socialism by making people’s lives better, and that’s what we’re trying to do every single day.
>
> — JD Vance, [New York Post](https://nypost.com/2026/08/14/us-news/jd-vance-warns-republicans-not-to-blame-young-americans-for-the-appeal-of-socialism/), 2026

The center-left and the center-right managed to find a compromise. The welfare state was expanded and the lives of white working people were improved just enough to appease the left and undermine the momentum of more radical movements that demanded structural reform. The oppression of the black population in the US was still severe enough to radicalize many into joining movements such as the Black Panthers, which was crushed by the FBI with the infamous assassinations of Fred Hampton and Mark Clark. But still, the successes of the civil rights movement were enough to give people some degree of hope that the system works and change is possible. As we reached the "[end of history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_End_of_History_and_the_Last_Man)" announced by Francis Fukuyama, with Western liberal democracy becoming the dominant political system around the world after the fall of the Soviet Union, terms like "anarchism", "anarcho-syndicalism", or even "libertarian socialism" became anachronistic. The specter of Communism was no longer haunting Europe, and revolution was no longer in the air.

Today, [economic democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Economic_democracy&oldid=1345662943) is the terminology commonly used by those who propose to "shift ownership and decision-making power from corporate shareholders and corporate managers (such as a board of directors) to a larger group of public stakeholders that includes workers, consumers, suppliers, communities and the broader public". Indeed, the Marcora Law and ESOPs mentioned earlier are examples of institutions that came into being via gradual, structural reform. And Mondragon shows the same can be achieved from below, having grown from [a single workshop founded in 1956](https://www.mondragon-corporation.com/en/history/) into a federation of cooperatives without any revolution, or even a sympathetic government.

In circles that embrace radical, revolutionary language, this terminology may be frowned upon. But overindulging in these linguistic debates risks distracting us from doing the work necessary to actually achieve any concrete victories, so I support the adoption of different strategies at the same time, regardless of the language behind them. If you live in an unstable country with a strong anarchist movement that could realistically overthrow the regime, then a revolutionary strategy might make sense. If you live in a country with a strong democracy and leaders who are potentially sympathetic to economic democracy, traditional activism and public pressure might be sufficient. In other contexts, a DIY approach might be the best way to go: creating "dual power" structures from the ground up, meaning grassroots institutions like cooperatives and mutual aid networks that operate in parallel with the state and can gradually replace it. Most countries don't fit any of these categories perfectly, so we'd probably benefit from taking inspiration from all strategies.

And in case "structural reform" sounds like a euphemism for timidity, it's worth remembering how radical reform can get. In 1975, Rudolf Meidner and Anna Hedborg, economists at LO, Sweden's blue-collar union confederation, [proposed requiring](https://onlabor.org/a-swedish-answer-to-labors-8-trillion-question/) every large Swedish company to issue new shares equivalent to 20% of its annual profits into union-controlled "wage-earner funds". Because the transfer was proportional to profits, the most successful firms would be socialized fastest: Volvo was projected to come under effective worker control within five years, and the Swedish economy as a whole to become majority worker-owned within a few decades. The LO congress adopted the plan unanimously in 1976, and Swedish business responded as if facing a revolution, staging on 4 October 1983 [one of the largest street protests](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00076791.2023.2298360) in the country's history. The version that passed parliament later that year was [heavily watered down](https://nordics.info/show/artikel/wage-earner-funds): five regional funds financed by a modest tax, capped at 8% ownership of any single company. Even those were dismantled after the conservatives came to power in 1991. Denmark's Social Democrats had proposed a similar scheme in 1973, which never passed, and the idea resurfaced as recently as 2019, when the UK Labour Party ran on [requiring every company](https://www.cliffordchance.com/briefings/2019/09/labour_s_plan_forthecompulsoryacquisitiono.html) with more than 250 employees to transfer 1% of its equity per year, up to a total of 10%, into an "Inclusive Ownership Fund" owned by its workers.

Personally, I strongly support policies of this kind, including ones as radical as the original Meidner Plan. Strictly speaking, these are wage-earner fund schemes rather than cooperative conversions: they create collective ownership through union- or employee-controlled funds, not one-member-one-vote firms. But they belong to the same family as the ESOPs and employee ownership trusts mentioned earlier, and they show what Gorz's non-reformist reform looks like in practice: piecemeal, legal, and reversible in method, revolutionary in ambition. A plan formally adopted by the mainstream labor movement of one of the world's most stable democracies would have transferred the bulk of the country's economy to its workers within a generation. If reformism has a reputation for timidity, it's not because reform is inherently timid, but because we have stopped proposing reforms of this magnitude.

Radical as it can be, though, legislation is only one end of the anarchist strategic spectrum. At the other end lies direct action, which is central to anarchist practice and often means clashing with the state: squatting unused buildings, planting community gardens without asking for permits, sabotaging things the movement considers harmful, from advertising billboards to surveillance cameras. To be fair, then, the association between anarchism and chaos discussed earlier is not entirely unfounded. Alongside this practical tradition there is a written one explicitly defending destruction. Bakunin declared in [1842](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/1842/reaction-germany.htm) that "the passion for destruction is a creative passion, too". Johann Most published what was [literally a manual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Science_of_Revolutionary_Warfare) of bomb-making and sabotage in 1885, and the theorists of "[propaganda of the deed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propaganda_of_the_deed)" inspired a wave of assassinations that killed a French president, a Spanish prime minister, an Italian king, and an American president between 1894 and 1901. The tradition survives in insurrectionary anarchism: Alfredo Bonanno's [*Armed Joy*](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/alfredo-m-bonanno-armed-joy) (1977) earned its author a prison sentence, and Peter Gelderloos's [*How Nonviolence Protects the State*](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/peter-gelderloos-how-nonviolence-protects-the-state) (2007) argues that property destruction and rioting are effective tactics unfairly stigmatized by the nonviolence norm.

Where do I stand? As a consequentialist, I can't reject these tactics axiomatically: I support direct action exactly to the extent that I believe it's effective, and Gelderloos at least has the virtue of arguing in those terms. What I do reject is the deontological shortcut some anarchists rely on: "the state is illegitimate, therefore its laws are illegitimate, therefore I'm entitled to break them." Whether or not the state's authority rests on solid philosophical foundations, laws and rules are legitimate to the extent that they prevent suffering, and many of them demonstrably do. And strategy matters, which means historical context matters too. The anarchist classics defending violence were written in a world where states were far more openly brutal, where political assassinations were common, and where armed resistance could still plausibly win popular support. The French army killed [thousands of Parisians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Commune) in a single week to crush the Paris Commune in 1871, and striking workers regularly faced soldiers rather than negotiators. In a developed country in the 21st century, even amid extreme inequality and democratic backsliding, the overwhelming majority *does* consider the state legitimate. Gratuitous transgression therefore doesn't read as revolution but as vandalism, and the media can easily use it to turn the public against the whole movement, dismissing anarchists as rebellious teenagers instead of engaging with their strongest arguments. This worry is not an outsider's smear, either. Murray Bookchin, one of the most influential anarchist thinkers of the twentieth century, spent his last decades attacking what he called "[lifestyle anarchism](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/murray-bookchin-social-anarchism-or-lifestyle-anarchism-an-unbridgeable-chasm)", singling out one of the scene's cult books for promoting a mood "in which disorganization is conceived as an art form and graffiti supplants programs".

This pragmatism extends to the state itself. As an anarchist I can see the appeal of a world where centralized power on the scale of the modern state didn't exist, but that is not the world we inherit, and refusing to use the tools available would be putting ideological purity above the reduction of suffering. Recall the Chomsky formulation: power is illegitimate *unless it proves itself legitimate*. Sometimes it does. Smallpox was eradicated by [a coordinated campaign](https://www.who.int/news-room/spotlight/history-of-vaccination/history-of-smallpox-vaccination#:~:text=the%20contributions%20of%20hundreds%20of,surveillance%20officers%20and%20health%20workers&text=two%20thirds%20of%20which%20came,for%20their%20own%20eradication%20efforts) of state health bureaucracies. China's dominance in solar panels, batteries, and electric vehicles was engineered through [subsidies and industrial planning](https://www.eco-business.com/news/the-new-three-how-china-came-to-lead-solar-cell-lithium-battery-and-ev-manufacturing/#:~:text=it%20subsidised%20these%20industries%20heavily%20and%20its%20competitors%20did%20not), and it is [the main reason](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-solar-became-the-cheapest-form-of-energy-in-the-world/#:~:text=The%20Chinese%20solar%20industry%20is,industry%20with%20smaller%20production%20lines.) solar energy became cheap enough to compete with fossil fuels everywhere else. And as the economist Mariana Mazzucato [has documented](https://time.com/4089171/mariana-mazzucato/#:~:text=The%20parts%20of%20the%20smart,advanced%20by%20the%20Defense%20Department), the internet, GPS, and [the research behind mRNA vaccines](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2020/dec/opinion-designing-vaccines-people-not-profits#:~:text=decades%20of%20massive%20public%20investment%20in%20research%20and%20development) were all funded by states, not markets. Indeed, most of the reforms I listed above, such as the Marcora Law, ESOPs, and employee ownership trusts, are themselves state interventions. Whether a given intervention reduces suffering is an empirical question like any other, to be answered case by case rather than settled in advance by a slogan about free markets or big government.

### Compatibility with scientific consequentialism

In the last few paragraphs, I have attempted to provide a brief description and history of the political/economic philosophy I have come to tentatively support. But why is this philosophy more compatible with scientific consequentialism than any others? That's what I will explain in the following sections.

#### Fallibilism

[Fallibilism](https://iep.utm.edu/fallibil/) is a philosophical position that acknowledges the fact that we can almost never know anything for sure. According to this view, all we can ever have are tentative hypotheses that we are only justified in holding with partial degrees of confidence. As I have argued before, this is a core value of a scientific epistemology. As [Popper and others have argued](https://iep.utm.edu/popp-pol/#:~:text=Democracy%20happens%20to%20be%20the,get%20rid%20of%20bad%20rulers), the beauty of democracy is exactly its role as a self-improving mechanism within a political context. What's the best possible political/economic system? Under a consequentialist framework, this is an empirical question that we will never have a final answer to. That's why whatever system we create needs to be easily updatable.

Economic democracy, therefore, should be even more compatible with the scientific ethos than the economic authoritarianism under which we live today. If democracy serves a positive role as a self-correction mechanism in the political realm, why wouldn't it be equally beneficial in the economic realm? In fact, that's how [many define anarchism](https://reason.com/2025/06/10/the-best-democracy-is-anarchy/): radically embracing democracy in all realms of life. Anarchists support a much more expanded, participatory, grassroots, decentralized democracy in the political realm, democratic deliberation in the workplace, [democratically run schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_school) in the educational realm, etc.

In radical leftist circles, many express skepticism towards Karl Popper, often accusing him of being a "liberal", yet another essentially contested term. In the US, "liberal" is roughly a synonym for center-left progressive, while in Europe and Latin America it more often refers to pro-free-market centrists or even the economic right. As I have mentioned earlier, part of this skepticism is directed towards the reformism implied by his views, which I have already responded to, but another part might also be related to his criticism of Karl Marx. Radical leftists, sometimes even in the US, tend to use the word "liberal" to describe moderates who support socially progressive welfare capitalism instead of structural reform and worker-centered politics. But again, none of that follows from Popperian philosophy.

Popper's criticism of Marx was aimed primarily at his method, not at his egalitarian ends. What he attacked was [historicist prophecy and wholesale utopian engineering](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/#:~:text=Historicism%20he%20identified%20as%20the,rules%20towards%20a%20determinate%20end&text=large%20scale%20social%20planning%20to%20an%20antecedently%20conceived%20blueprint%20is%20inherently%20misconceived): predicting the future course of history from supposed laws of historical development, then redesigning society wholesale to match. In [*Unended Quest*](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/420091-i-remained-a-socialist-for-several-years-even-after-my) he describes remaining a socialist for several years after abandoning Marxism: "[I]f there could be such a thing as socialism combined with individual liberty, I would be a socialist still. For nothing could be better than living a modest, simple, and free life in an egalitarian society." He eventually dismissed this as "no more than a beautiful dream", on the grounds that "the attempt to realize equality endangers freedom". [*The Open Society*](http://www.the-rathouse.com/OpenSocietyOnLIne/Chapter-20-Capitalism-and-its-Fate.html#:~:text=how%20justified%20he%20was%20in,the%20hell%20of%20unrestrained%20capitalism) itself explicitly grants that Marx's account of unrestrained capitalism's injustice was substantially right for his time. Popper's ["paradox of freedom" argument](http://www.the-rathouse.com/OpenSocietyOnLIne/Chapter17-Legal-Social-System.html#:~:text=We%20must%20demand%20that%20unrestrained%20capitalism%20give%20way%20to%20an%20economic%20interventionism) runs as follows: unlimited economic freedom lets the economically strong coerce the weak, so the state must intervene. He says plainly that laissez-faire capitalism must give way to what he calls economic interventionism.

It may be true that in Popperian/rationalist circles there are many people that identify as liberals and who support welfare capitalism and reject labels such as socialism and communism, but these positions don't follow necessarily from the Popperian arguments I am relying on. Making that assumption would be falling prey to the fallacy of guilt by association. On the contrary, as I have argued in this section, it is by no means a stretch to derive anarchist conclusions from his philosophy. As an evidence-based consequentialist, however, I don't think any political system can be properly defended a priori from the armchair. So what empirical data do we have to justify our confidence in the belief that economic democracy would be good for the world?

#### Evidence

Two works were particularly important in convincing me that workplace democracy delivers concrete benefits: Richard Wolff's [*Democracy at Work: A Cure for Capitalism*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/13591849-democracy-at-work) and the documentary [*Can We Do It Ourselves?*](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt4842618/), directed by Patrik Witkowsky and released in 2015. In the book, Wolff proposes converting capitalist firms into "worker self-directed enterprises": companies where the workers who produce the surplus also decide, democratically, how it gets distributed. His flagship example is Mondragon, which I mentioned earlier. After visiting the corporation in May 2012, Wolff reported that a democratically adopted rule limits the highest-paid members to earning [6.5 times](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2012/jun/24/alternative-capitalism-mondragon#:~:text=limits%20top%2Dpaid%20worker%2Fmembers%20to%20earning%206.5%20times%20the%20lowest%2Dpaid%20workers) what the lowest-paid earn, whereas in US corporations CEOs can expect to be paid [400 times](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2012/jun/24/alternative-capitalism-mondragon#:~:text=CEOs%20can%20expect%20to%20be%20paid%20400%20times%20an%20average%20worker%27s%20salary) an average worker's salary. He was also impressed by Mondragon's commitment to job security: instead of resorting to layoffs during downturns, the corporation [moves workers](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2012/jun/24/alternative-capitalism-mondragon#:~:text=created%20a%20system%20to%20move,to%20those%20needing%20more%20workers) from cooperatives that need fewer of them to cooperatives that need more, retraining them when necessary.

The documentary approaches the same question from a Swedish perspective, interviewing philosophers, economists, and political scientists on both sides of the debate (including Noam Chomsky, David Schweickart, and Bo Rothstein) and visiting cooperatives that compete successfully in ordinary markets. Its central argument is that we have no good reason to keep accepting authoritarian rule in the workplace after having rejected it everywhere else. But anecdotes about successful cooperatives, however inspiring, can always be dismissed as cherry-picking. What does the systematic evidence say?

In 2016, Virginie Pérotin, professor of economics at Leeds University Business School, reviewed two decades of international data on worker cooperatives in Europe, the US, and Latin America, and concluded that they are [more productive](https://www.democracyatwork.info/what_do_we_really_know_about_worker_cooperatives#:~:text=worker%20cooperatives%20are%20more%20productive%20than%20conventional%20businesses) than conventional businesses and [survive at least as long](https://www.democracyatwork.info/what_do_we_really_know_about_worker_cooperatives#:~:text=Worker%20co%2Doperatives%20survive%20at%20least,and%20have%20more%20stable%20employment), with more stable employment. The broader literature on employee ownership points in the same direction. Summarizing over 100 studies, the Rutgers economist Douglas Kruse reports that publicly traded US companies with employee ownership [are about 20% more likely](https://wol.iza.org/articles/does-employee-ownership-improve-performance/long#:~:text=Publicly%20traded%20US%20companies%20with,survive%20over%20a%2012%2Dyear%20period) than comparable firms to survive over a 12-year period, that closely held companies with employee ownership are [only half as likely](https://wol.iza.org/articles/does-employee-ownership-improve-performance/long#:~:text=closely%20held%20companies%20with%20employee,go%20bankrupt%20or%20close%20down) to go bankrupt or close down, and that the layoff rate among employee owners is [less than half](https://wol.iza.org/articles/does-employee-ownership-improve-performance/long#:~:text=the%20reported%20layoff%20rate%20among,the%20rate%20of%20other%20employees) the rate of other employees. These benefits are not paid for by the workers themselves either, since employee ownership [tends to come on top](https://wol.iza.org/articles/does-employee-ownership-improve-performance/long#:~:text=employee%20ownership%20tends%20to%20come%20on%20top%20of%20market%20levels%20of%20pay) of market levels of pay. And consistent with the claims about worker satisfaction, employee owners generally have [lower turnover and absenteeism](https://wol.iza.org/articles/does-employee-ownership-improve-performance/long#:~:text=employee%20owners%20generally%20have%20lower,greater%20willingness%20to%20work%20hard), more company pride and loyalty, and greater willingness to work hard. These effects seem to depend, however, on combining ownership with genuine participation in decision-making, which is exactly what distinguishes workplace democracy from a mere financial perk.

But if worker cooperatives are more productive than conventional firms and survive at least as long, why are they still so rare? Economists have puzzled over this question for decades (a well-known article by the law professor Peter Molk is literally titled "[The Puzzling Lack of Cooperatives](https://www.tulanelawreview.org/pub/volume88/issue5/the-puzzling-lack-of-cooperatives)"), and the answer has less to do with how co-ops perform than with how businesses get funded. When you buy shares in a conventional company, you are buying a bundle of three things: a vote in how the company is run, a slice of its profits, and the right to resell your stake later at a higher price if the company grows. That bundle is what [venture capitalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venture_capital) are gambling on when they pour money into risky, unprofitable startups: most of their bets fail, but the occasional Google or Amazon pays back the initial investment a hundred times over. We have smartphones and AI, the argument goes, because capitalism harnesses greed, and a cooperative simply has nothing equivalent to sell: its votes belong to the workers, one each, regardless of capital, and since a co-op can never be acquired or taken public against its members' will, there is no jackpot to cash in. Whatever a co-op offers an investor will look less like a lottery ticket and more like a bond: your money back, plus a modest return.

Raising capital as a co-op is genuinely harder, then. But it would be defeatist to conclude that [there is no alternative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_is_no_alternative), because cooperatives have been inventing workarounds for decades. Equal Exchange, an American fair-trade coffee co-op, has funded its growth by selling [non-voting preferred shares](https://shop.equalexchange.coop/pages/invest#:~:text=outside%20preferred%20Class%20B%20non%2Dvoting%20shares) to outside investors, which pay a yearly [target dividend of 5%](https://www.start.coop/case-studies/equal-exchange#:~:text=Each%20share%20purchased%20offers%20investors%20a%20yearly%205%25) but confer no control whatsoever. France builds outside investment directly into the legal form of the co-op: investors can become non-working members of a SCOP, but they are [capped at 35% of the votes](https://coops4dev.coop/sites/default/files/2021-09/France%20Legal%20Framework%20Analysis%20National%20Report.pdf), so workers always keep control. Mondragon attacked the problem from the supply side, founding its own cooperative bank, [Caja Laboral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laboral_Kutxa#:~:text=was%20founded%20in%201959%20by%20Jos%C3%A9%20Mar%C3%ADa%20Arizmendiarrieta), in 1959 precisely so that the young cooperatives around it would have a financial instrument of their own. There are even hybrid models: the Irish dairy co-op Kerry [floated its business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerry_Group#:~:text=floated%20on%20the%20Irish%20Stock,price%20of%2052p%20per%20share) on the stock exchange in 1986 as Kerry Group plc, raising money from ordinary shareholders while the co-op kept a [majority of the shares](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/kerry-group-plc#:~:text=from%2052%20percent%20to%2039%20percent) for the following decade. And new instruments keep appearing, such as [revenue-based financing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revenue-based_financing), where an investor advances money in exchange for a percentage of the company's future revenues until they have received an agreed multiple of their investment: a genuine bet on the company's growth that never touches ownership or control. None of this replicates the scale of venture capital, and tech co-ops remain rare, though not nonexistent: Igalia, a worker cooperative from Galicia, is [one of the biggest contributors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igalia#:~:text=In%202019%20they%20were%20the,the%20WebKit%20and%20Chromium%20codebases) to the browser engines behind Chrome and Safari. But the funding gap is a problem of financial engineering and public policy, not a law of nature, and legislation like the Marcora Law shows that policy can close at least part of it.

The evidence on health is more indirect, but it comes from some of the most influential studies in modern epidemiology. The [Whitehall studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitehall_Study) have followed tens of thousands of British civil servants since 1967, and found a steep social gradient in health: men in the lowest employment grades had a [three-fold higher mortality rate](https://unhealthywork.org/classic-studies/the-whitehall-study/#:~:text=three%2Dfold%20higher%20mortality%20rate%20than%20men%20in%20the%20highest%20grade) than men in the highest grade. Since even the lowest-ranking civil servants were stably employed office workers, poverty alone couldn't explain the gradient. The second Whitehall study, led by the epidemiologist Michael Marmot, identified lack of autonomy as a major culprit: low control in the work environment is associated with an [increased risk](https://unhealthywork.org/classic-studies/the-whitehall-study/#:~:text=Low%20control%20in%20the%20work,women%20employed%20in%20government%20offices) of coronary heart disease, and much of the health gradient between ranks can be attributed to differences in the psychosocial work environment. If low control over one's own work damages health, then extending democratic control to the workplace is not just a matter of fairness or efficiency, but potentially of public health.

Finally, another argument for democratic firms is that they should find it harder to engage in unethical practices, since a small elite can't push a harmful agenda without the support of the workers. The clearest evidence for this comes from finance. According to a 2013 study published by the International Labour Organization, financial cooperatives [outperformed investor-owned banks](https://www.ilo.org/resource/article/financial-cooperatives-safe-bet-crisis#:~:text=out%2Dperformed%20traditional%20investor%2Downed%20banks%20during,on%20almost%20every%20rating%20level) during the global financial crisis on almost every rating level: they entered the crisis with [higher stability ratings](https://www.ilo.org/resource/article/financial-cooperatives-safe-bet-crisis#:~:text=the%20cooperative%20banks%20had%20a%20higher%20average%20stability%20rating), maintained good credit ratings, and grew their assets and customer base while conventional banks collapsed or froze. As the study explains, banks driven by the need to maximize shareholder profits are led to [take much bigger investment risks](https://www.ilo.org/resource/article/financial-cooperatives-safe-bet-crisis#:~:text=take%20much%20bigger%20investment%20risks) (the scenario that triggered the 2007-2008 crisis in the first place), while customer-owned banks have no incentive to gamble with their members' deposits. Still, this argument should not be overstated. In a 2025 paper, the philosopher Joseph Heath argues that cooperatives are [not inherently more virtuous](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1470594X251387579) than corporations, pointing out, for example, that the plywood worker cooperatives of the Pacific Northwest lobbied against environmental protections for old-growth forests just like their capitalist competitors. Democracy internalizes harms to whoever is inside the demos: worker control reliably protects workers, but a cooperative's members can be just as tempted as shareholders to externalize costs onto outsiders. To me, however, this is less an argument against economic democracy than an argument for widening the demos, which is exactly what the definition quoted earlier proposes when it includes not only workers but also "consumers, suppliers, communities and the broader public".

The last argument I want to make is more speculative, but I find it too interesting to leave out. It comes from [evolutionary mismatch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_mismatch): the idea that traits which evolved as adaptations to ancestral environments can become harmful when the environment changes faster than evolution can track. For the overwhelming majority of our species' history, humans lived as nomadic hunter-gatherers, in small communities where survival depended on cooperation and where power was kept firmly in check. In [*Hierarchy in the Forest*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/2131522.Hierarchy_in_the_Forest), the anthropologist Christopher Boehm surveyed ethnographies of foraging societies from all over the world and concluded that their egalitarianism is actively maintained through what he calls a "reverse dominance hierarchy": the rank and file form coalitions to keep would-be alphas in check, using tools that range from gossip and ridicule to shunning and expulsion. Anarchists have been making arguments in this spirit for over a century: Kropotkin's [*Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/4341), published in 1902, was an early attempt to show, against the social Darwinists of his day, that cooperation has been as central to our evolution as competition. More recently, in [*A Hunter-Gatherer's Guide to the 21st Century*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/56883406-a-hunter-gatherer-s-guide-to-the-21st-century), the evolutionary biologists Heather Heying and Bret Weinstein argue that many modern pathologies stem from the "hyper-novelty" of an environment that is now changing faster than our minds and bodies can adapt.

Industrial society replaced that world of small, self-governing communities with one of anonymous cities, hierarchical workplaces, and centralized bureaucracies that rarely consult the people affected by their decisions, an environment radically unlike the one our social instincts evolved for. The consequences are becoming hard to ignore. In 2023, the US Surgeon General published an advisory titled ["Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation"](https://www.npr.org/2023/05/02/1173418268/loneliness-connection-mental-health-dementia-surgeon-general), warning that lacking social connection raises the risk of premature death to levels comparable to smoking 15 cigarettes a day. In 2025, the WHO Commission on Social Connection reported that [1 in 6 people worldwide](https://www.who.int/news/item/30-06-2025-social-connection-linked-to-improved-heath-and-reduced-risk-of-early-death#:~:text=1%20in%206%20people%20worldwide%20is%20affected%20by%20loneliness) is affected by loneliness, which is linked to an estimated [100 deaths every hour](https://www.who.int/news/item/30-06-2025-social-connection-linked-to-improved-heath-and-reduced-risk-of-early-death#:~:text=100%20deaths%20every%20hour). Researchers in evolutionary psychiatry have proposed that rising rates of depression may likewise be a ["disease of modernity"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3330161/), driven by lifestyles that leave us increasingly [sedentary, sleep-deprived, and socially isolated](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3330161/#:~:text=Modern%20populations%20are%20increasingly%20overfed%2C%20malnourished%2C%20sedentary%2C%20sunlight%2Ddeficient%2C%20sleep%2Ddeprived%2C%20and%20socially%2Disolated). If the mismatch framework is right, the communal principles of social anarchism (small self-governing communities, mutual aid, face-to-face deliberation) are a return to the social environment our brains are adapted to, and we should expect them to benefit mental health, social cohesion, and democratic participation. To be clear, this line of research is young: nobody has directly tested whether anarchist forms of organization improve mental health, and evolutionary hypotheses about behavior are notoriously hard to falsify. But the fact that the research is young is not by itself a reason to dismiss it, and the mismatch between our evolved sociality and the world we have built is one of the best-documented facts about modern life.

None of this settles the question definitively. Most of these studies compare firms within capitalist economies, and nobody has run the experiment of democratizing an entire modern economy. Consistent with the fallibilism defended above, I hold these conclusions tentatively. But that is exactly why the reformist path described earlier is so attractive: expanding worker ownership through legislation like the Marcora Law is a piecemeal experiment in the Popperian sense, one that improves the lives of the workers involved while generating precisely the data we need to decide how much further to go.

## Conclusion

It's hard to define precisely what a political ideology even means, but in practice it is [usually understood](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/7513957-here-s-a-simple-definition-of-ideology-a-set-of-beliefs) as “a set of beliefs about the proper order of society and how it can be achieved”. As I have argued, however, these sets of beliefs are often a messy mix of moral and empirical assumptions that are treated as axioms rather than tentative conclusions. Worse still, these bundles of assumptions are often grouped together not because of some shared essence, but as a result of cultural and historical accidents. Embracing one political ideology over another and adopting their language and branding is often an exercise in tribalism rather than moral clarity.

Unfortunately, however, in the absence of an alternative linguistic framework, it's very hard to communicate about political issues without resorting to some of these labels. If I am to adopt any labels to describe myself, I'd say I'm a structural reformist and a social anarchist. In this article I have focused on employee ownership and workplace democracy because that's what distinguishes anarchism from both "capitalism" and "communism" as generally understood. However, there are many policies that I supported long before I went down the anarchist rabbit hole because they follow from my scientific epistemology and my consequentialist ethics:

1. Tax justice – The rich should pay more than the poor in effective tax rates because, partly due to diminishing returns, relieving the suffering of the poor does much more good than maintaining the privileges of the rich.
2. Basic income – Nobody should be deprived of the basic needs for a dignified life.
3. Limitarianism – Individual wealth should be capped at roughly $10 million. The level of wealth accumulation accepted today is fundamentally incompatible with the principles of democracy. Nobody should be so wealthy that they can buy off politicians and manipulate the public via million-dollar campaigns.
4. LGBT+ rights – Same-sex unions should be legal because they don't harm anybody. All legal discrimination against LGBT+ folk should end immediately, and social discrimination should be discouraged by whatever means are proven effective.
5. Abortion rights – Abortion should be legal because [it is harmless](https://medium.com/humanist-voices/abortion-is-harmless-62ddebdd4f72). Fetuses are not sentient until very late in pregnancy.
6. Sex-work should be decriminalized – Sending vulnerable women to jail for sex work doesn't minimize suffering in any way. Even the Nordic model, which only criminalizes the purchase of sexual services and not the selling, has been [shown empirically](https://www.lse.ac.uk/research/research-for-the-world/politics/criminalising-the-sex-buyer#:~:text=sex%20workers%20are%20pushed%20into%20ever%20more%20precarious%20environments&text=96%20per%20cent%20of%20those,and%20more%20vulnerable%20to%20exploitation) to drive sex workers underground, where they are more vulnerable to abuse.
7. Drug decriminalization – Treating drug use as a criminal justice problem rather than a public health one is [known to cause](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/03/160324104516.htm#:~:text=serious%20detrimental%20effects%20on%20the%20health%2C%20wellbeing,of%20drug%20users%20and%20the%20wider%20public&text=no%20significant%20increase%20in%20problematic%20drug%20use) all sorts of societal harms without actually mitigating the harms caused by drugs.

Most would label these views as broadly "leftist" and not necessarily anarchist. But ideologies overlap. Somebody can support all the policies above but call themselves a socialist, a communist, or even a liberal. These policies are compatible with anarchism because they're fundamentally anti-authoritarian and promote a more equal distribution of power, but they're also compatible with other ideologies. That's why I prefer to focus on policies rather than labels. Even a broad label like "leftist" makes me uneasy, especially because I also have many views that are extremely frowned upon in leftist circles:

1. My arguments for legal abortions are utilitarian, and I reject the deontological arguments based on natural rights that rest behind popular leftist slogans such as "my body my choice".
2. I find slogans like "trans women are women" [performative](https://medium.com/humanist-voices/performative-language-a466e9aee860) and devoid of factual meaning, and I have mixed feelings about many of the mainstream narratives on trans issues, as I explain in "[Who decides what’s transphobic?](https://arielpontes.medium.com/who-decides-whats-transphobic-2620846de301)".
3. I don't think the "ironic misandry" popular in some online feminist spaces (#KillAllMen jokes, "male tears" mugs) is productive for the feminist movement.
4. I believe that cancel culture is a real phenomenon, that it is particularly endemic in leftist circles, and that it is harmful.
5. I believe that entrepreneurship is a good thing and that it should be encouraged and never ignored in a political program. I believe it is possible to cultivate ambition and competitiveness in a healthy way, harnessing them to benefit society as a whole.

As much as it may be impossible to avoid ideological language completely, we should all reserve the right to pick and choose our beliefs on the basis of the evidence supporting them, and not how they're bundled together with other beliefs under one label or another. When debating a self-proclaimed supporter of capitalism or socialism, for example, ask yourself: are we debating policy or labels? Humans are tribal creatures. Sometimes the best strategy to persuade people is to translate our beliefs into a language that they can understand without rejecting it outright with a knee-jerk reaction. We should keep our identity small, use these labels sparingly, and try not to take it personally when people are skeptical or dismissive of labels that we hold dear. If we want to build a big tent political movement to save democracy from oligarchs, we can't afford to be splintered and polarized, and I don't think this goal is fundamentally left or right.
