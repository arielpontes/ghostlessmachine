I first remember becoming aware of ideologies in adolescence, when I started questioning my family’s religious beliefs. That sent me down a rabbit hole that eventually made me realize I couldn’t believe in any claims without evidence, so I became an atheist. It’s hard to overstate how formative this experience was for me. My deepest and most foundational beliefs were shattered almost overnight, as was the trust I had in my family as a reliable source of information.

My family was never devout in any traditional sense: they never took me to church or force any traditions on me. Still, they were very mystical and when I came out I got a lot of pushback. In fact, I got pushback everywhere: in school, online, from my peers. I was often accused of being insensitive, which quickly made me very aware of the tribal nature of religion. No matter how diplomatic I tried to be, I quickly realized that the only way not to upset people was to pretend I’m not an atheist, something that I refused to do. Dissent is costly, but I was willing to pay the price.

After losing faith, my interest in spirituality naturally transitioned to an interest in philosophy, particularly epistemology and ethics: what does it mean to know something? Can we really claim to know anything at all? What does it mean to be good? How can we make the world a better place? One of the biggest shocks I had during this journey was realizing how profoundly public policy still reflects people’s religious beliefs. Both abortion and same-sex marriage were illegal in Brazil at that time for blatantly religious reasons. Abortion is still illegal today, and I am still horrified by all the suffering this ban causes.

## Ethics

As my epistemic and moral values started taking shape, I started to get increasingly opinionated about policy. Brazil is a secular state on paper, so if you’re going to ban abortions, you need a better argument than “life is sacred”. As an atheist, I was often asked why I wouldn’t rape and murder, and my first answer was “because I don’t want to”. But I couldn’t help but wonder: what if I did want to? Would I just do it? It didn’t take much thinking to conclude that I wouldn’t. After all, I may not have antisocial urges as extreme as rape and murder, but that doesn’t mean I’ve never had a problematic impulse. We can all remember one situation or another when we had an opportunity to steal and get away with it, to lie in order to get ahead, or even to abuse someone weaker out of anger or frustration. Sometimes we may give in to such temptations, but not always. We don’t always do whatever we feel like, even if we know we can get away with it. How come?

Nobody likes to be lied to. Nobody likes to be cheated on or treated unfairly. Nobody likes to suffer or experience unnecessary discomfort. If I expect to be treated with respect and dignity, I should also treat others with respect and dignity. This is the Golden Rule: treat others as you’d want to be treated. It’s found under one form or another in almost all cultures. It is basically the Copernican principle applied to ethics. In the words of Henry Sidgwick:

> [T]he good of any one individual is of no more importance, from the point of view… of the Universe, than the good of any other;
>
> — Henry Sidgwick, [*The Methods of Ethics*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/46743), 1874

This chain of reasoning soon led to the conclusion, therefore, that making the world a better place essentially means minimizing the amount of unnecessary suffering in the world. Some may criticize this view as simplistic or “utilitarian”. I don’t necessarily reject the label of “utilitarian”, and I’ve written a series of articles refuting arguments against utilitarianism and consequentialism more broadly. However, you don’t have to be a utilitarian to agree with me. You just have to agree, as virtually everybody does, that minimizing suffering is one of the things that matters most when talking about ethics, even if it’s not the only one.

I have defended utilitarianism against alternative moral frameworks for years. This was the main theme of my master’s thesis. But the more I studied these alternatives, the more I started to see them not as alternative moral positions, and simply as different moral languages. Different approaches to ethics can reveal genuinely important insights. Deontology reveals the importance of sticking to good rules instead of trying to do complex utilitarian calculations every time you need to make a moral decision. Virtue ethics highlights the importance of cultivating virtues so we can make good decisions relying on muscle memory rather than careful analysis. But what is a good rule if not one that, if respected, leads to a world with less suffering? And what is a virtue if not a trait that, when cultivated, leads to happier communities? My point is: any moral framework that tries to derive moral truths from first principles can ultimately be described in utilitarian language. But if one moral language can be translated to another, what’s the point of using utilitarian language? This brings us to the next topic.

## Epistemology

The biggest advantage of utilitarian language is that it allows us to reduce any moral question to an empirical one. But how can we answer empirical questions with confidence? That’s the question epistemology tries to answer. And what answers has it found? Philosophy is, of course, a perpetual debate that is never settled definitely. However, after centuries of studying and applying different epistemic strategies to the problems facing humanity, it is safe to say that we have learned some lessons.

Science is the most effective method ever devised for collectively converging on a shared set of beliefs at the global level, regardless of cultural background. Some criticize science for being a Western, white, or male ideology, but science has a rich and diverse history, and those who employ its methods today are able to create technologies that compete or surpass those created by white Western men, regardless of race, gender, or cultural origin.

Science works because it respects certain values, such as:

- Epistemic humility: never be too confident about your beliefs, and always leave room for correction.
- Fallibilism/Falsificationism: always formulate your hypotheses such that they can be experimentally falsified at least in principle.
- Bayesianism: always update your confidence levels in response to new data.
- The Copernican principle (broadly interpreted): none of us is special. We’re all vulnerable to cognitive biases and logical fallacies, so we must all find mechanisms to correct our own errors.

In order to respect these principles, science relies on mechanisms such as replicable experiments, randomized controlled trials, peer review, and adversarial collaboration. But perhaps the most important insight of a scientific epistemology, the common thread tying all these values together, is simply the recognition of the obvious fact that knowledge comes in degrees. We can’t justify having 100% confidence in almost any assumption. What we call scientific knowledge is simply our best guess of how the world works at any given time. Following the Cartesian tradition, the only thing I would claim to know with full confidence is that I am a sentient being: I experience subjective states of consciousness that have a negative or positive valence. Anything else is a belief that I hold with a lower or higher degree of confidence. After all, I could be dreaming, hallucinating, or stuck in a simulation. Once we acknowledge that, it’s not too hard to take one step further and realize that most of the time we’re not justified in having much any confidence at all in most of our beliefs, a view sometimes called epistemic pessimism or skepticism.

## Politics

As I have argued above, I adopt a consequentialist approach to ethics and an evidence-based, scientific approach to epistemology. This reduces any question about policy to an empirical one: which of the policies being discussed will in fact lead to a better world with less suffering? If these epistemic and moral principles are sufficient for me to form an opinion on any policy issue, what is my political ideology? Is there any room for political ideologies in my worldview at all?

My instinct is to say that no, there isn’t any room for political ideologies in an epistemically and morally coherent worldview. Political ideologies are a messy mix of tacit empirical and moral assumptions. They are more similar to religion in their dogmatism and tribalism than they are to coherent and well-examined philosophical positions. Indeed, as Hyrum and Verlan Lewis argue in [The Myth of Left and Right (2023)](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/62871369-the-myth-of-left-and-right):

> Left–right ideologies are bundles of unrelated political positions connected by nothing other than a group. A conservative or liberal is not someone who has a conservative or liberal philosophy, but someone who belongs to the conservative or liberal tribe. This means that ideologies do not define tribes, tribes define ideologies; ideology is not about what (worldviews), it is about who (groups); there is no liberalism or conservatism, only liberals and conservatives; and the political spectrum does not model an essential value, but only tribes and what they stand for at a specific time and place.

Indeed, that explains why even academics can’t agree on what “capitalism” or “socialism” means precisely. That’s why W.B. Gallie calls them [*essentially contested concepts*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essentially_contested_concept). I have talked to reasonable people who defend “capitalism” because the decentralized nature of free markets creates an environment where different businesses can compete and evolve under a Darwinian logic, leading to a “Cambrian explosion” of creativity that breeds innovation and technological breakthroughs, many of which can eradicate diseases, extend our life expectancy, or otherwise improve our quality of life. This is essentially the argument made by academics like [Joseph Schumpeter](https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/CreativeDestruction.html), who described capitalist innovation as a process of “creative destruction”, and [Friedrich Hayek](https://www.econlib.org/library/Essays/hykKnw.html), who argued that decentralized markets outperform central planning because they make use of knowledge that is dispersed across society.

I have also talked to anti-capitalists who would argue that none of the above is fundamentally “capitalistic”, and that such free-markets would continue to breed innovation under a system of collective ownership where all companies are worker-owned, democratically run cooperatives. This position also has its academic defenders: market socialists like [David Schweickart](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/918139.After_Capitalism) and [John Roemer](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/1354112.A_Future_for_Socialism) defend precisely such a system of worker-owned firms competing in free markets, and left-wing market anarchists like Kevin Carson go as far as calling their position “[free market anti-capitalism](https://mutualist.blogspot.com/)”. Even [Deirdre McCloskey](https://www.deirdremccloskey.com/docs/pdf/McCloskey_HowGrowthHappens.pdf), one of the most enthusiastic academic defenders of free markets, agrees that innovation has little to do with the accumulation of capital, and proposes replacing the “highly misleading word ‘capitalism’” with “innovism”. Who has the right definition of capitalism? Who has the authority to settle such semantic disputes? Nobody.

Unfortunately, few people have the humility to admit this. Most people think they know the one correct definition of these terms, even though they define their own ideology very positively and their opponents' very negatively. In the end, each side ends up dismissing the other as if they're naively misunderstanding their views or dishonestly misrepresentating it. That's why I don't identify as an anti-capitalist or an anti-communist. If we're willing to interpret people charitably, we'll see that many self-described capitalists are just uneasy about top-down, centralized, planned economies, while many self-described communists are just uneasy about the extremely unequal distribution of power in our current system.

For all the reasons mentioned so far, I have avoided politics for most of my life, even though I have always held very strong opinions about how to make the world a better place. Instead of joining a political movement, therefore, I focused on collaborating with civil society organizations and single-issue movements that shared my epistemic and moral values, such as Secular Humanism and Effective Altruism. In recent years, however, I have become increasingly uncomfortable with the supposed "apolitical" nature of these movements. As a pragmatist, I understand that sometimes it is necessary to avoid polarizing topics within a movement in order to focus on common goals. However, the shift towards oligarchy evidenced in Trumps's second term was so blatant that it became difficult to overlook. It is difficult to fight for social change when democracy itself has been hijacked by oligarchs. Today, 1% of the world's population owns x% of the world's wealth. This level of economic inequality is too extreme to be ignored. It's impossible to have an honest conversation about eliminating poverty while completely avoiding the topic of wealth redistribution. The idea that wealth redistribution is a "political" topic that should be avoided is fundamentally problematic. Whether redistribution is necessary to minimize suffering in the near term is an empirical question that should be answered scientifically like any other. If the ideological communities where I am active are too reluctant to take clear stances on these issues, I have no choice but to join explicitly political movements.

## Steelmanning political ideologies

Could it really be that all political ideologies are nothing but tribal nonsense though? There are, after all, generations of respected intellectuals who have defended and criticized a variety of political ideologies, written extremely influential books, and inspired revolutions around the world. Could it really be that they’re just tribal, dogmatic cultists who are confused about their own ethics and epistemology? I am a strong believer in the principle of charity, so before I dismiss all this intellectual history, I should at least try to make my best case in favor of political ideologies.

As a consequentialist, the only way I can make sense of a political ideology is if I assume that it is intended as a project whose goal is to create a society with minimal suffering. Indeed, that would explain the diversity of political ideologies humanity has come up with. One of the humbling implications of epistemic pessimism combined with consequentialism is that we are rarely justified in making any confident claim about morality at all. The truth is: political science is not physics. We can study public policy and comparative history all we want, but we’re never gonna be able to isolate all variables and demonstrate empirically that a certain policy will always have the same results in all contexts. Politics is chaotic. The butterfly effect ensures that a tiny difference in context can cause a policy to work in one place and fail in another, even if both are extremely similar.

But does that mean we should give up on studying policy altogether? Of course not. We just need to adjust our expectations and admit that all we have is a best guess of which policy is gonna be the most positive given our best models and the evidence we’ve been able to collect so far. This means we should take any political ideology with a grain of salt. Even if you’re a communist with a very concrete and detailed plan for how your communist utopia should work, you also need to have a plan for updating it in response to the new data that is generated every day as the system is stress tested by real life. Unfortunately, political ideologies rarely make such provisions.

To use Karl Popper’s terminology, we should strive for an open society: one where our assumptions are always open to revision and our institutions always open to change:

> Aestheticism and radicalism must lead us to jettison reason, and to replace it by a desperate hope for political miracles. This irrational attitude which springs from intoxication with dreams of a beautiful world is what I call Romanticism. It may seek its heavenly city in the past or in the future; it may preach ‘back to nature’ or ‘forward to a world of love and beauty’; but its appeal is always to our emotions rather than to reason. Even with the best intentions of making heaven on earth it only succeeds in making it a hell — that hell which man alone prepares for his fellow-men.
>
> — Karl Popper, [*The Open Society and Its Enemies*](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/1036399-aestheticism-and-radicalism-must-lead-us-to-jettison-reason-and), 1945

That’s why Popper defends “piecemeal” rather than “utopian” social engineering. Utopianism is simply so risky that it amounts to gambling with the future of humankind. Some may accuse Popper of being a “conservative” or “reformist”, but using these terms as insults is uncharitable. There is a spectrum between conservative or reformist on the one hand, and progressivist or revolutionary on the other. We all fall on different points in this spectrum depending on the issue under discussion. As Aristotle reminds us, virtue is the golden mean between two vices. We can debate the merits of conservatism and progressivism all day, but it shouldn’t be hard to agree that, as much as excessive stagnation can be a problem, too much change too fast can also have its social costs. And to be clear, I’m talking about the kind of Hobbesian/Burkean conservatism that prioritizes stability and the preservation of institutions in order to prevent bloodshed, not the kind of religious conservatism that focuses on controlling individual behavior.

As much as an excessively reformist attitude can lack ambition and crush any hopes of structural improvements in the lives of the many, an excessively revolutionary attitude can be naive and offer false hopes while failing to deliver any material improvements at all in the short term. This might explain why André Gorz felt the need to coin the term “non-reformist reform” to describe the kind of structural reform that doesn’t only focus on improving people’s lives within the current system, but on actually changing the current system meaningfully in order to achieve lasting, systemic improvements to our well being. When Rosa Luxemburg wrote “*Social Reform or Revolution?*” in 1899, revolution was a realistic option and Friedrich Ebert’s reformism was a real obstacle which eventually proved fatal to her revolutionary ideals and paved the way to the Third Reich. But as traumatic as this story may be, the current political climate is simply incomparable. Although it is true that Popperian arguments are often used to defend liberalism and capital-friendly reformism, they can be used equally well to defend more radical, structural reformism.

## Pseudoscientific or deontological ideologies

So if you also have scientific and consequentialist tendencies, which of the existing political ideologies would make most sense to adopt? Perhaps it’s easier to start with what political ideologies should be rejected for going directly against science. “Neoliberalism” is one of those essentially contested concepts that I usually try to avoid. However, I think it’s useful enough here to justify a quick working definition. According to [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Neoliberalism&oldid=1369666381):

> Neoliberalism is a political and economic ideology that advocates for free-market capitalism, and that became dominant in policy-making from the late 20th century onward. […] [It] is often associated with a set of economic liberalization policies, including lower taxes, privatization, deregulation, depoliticisation, consumer choice, labor market flexibilization, economic globalization, free trade, monetarism, austerity, and reductions in government spending. These policies are designed to increase the role of the private sector in the economy and society. Additionally, the neoliberal project is oriented towards the establishment of institutions and is inherently political in nature, extending beyond mere economic considerations.

There are a few aspects to neoliberalism that are worth noting because they make it fundamentally incompatible with science:

1. It presents itself as [apolitical and technocratic](https://www.policyalternatives.ca/news-research/after-neoliberalism-a-progressive-globalization/), even though it never reflected academic consensus.
2. Thanks in part to this ingenious framing, it succeeded in [influencing policy across the political spectrum](https://www.thenation.com/article/politics/third-way-dlc-bill-clinton-tony-blair-1990s-politics/) after the end of the cold war, shifting the political discourse towards [culture wars instead of class struggle](https://www.dissentmagazine.org/online_articles/progressive-neoliberalism-reactionary-populism-nancy-fraser/).
3. Its network of conservative and right-libertarian organizations, political parties, and think tanks, are [very well funded by billionaires and oligarchs](https://newrepublic.com/article/175488/atlas-shadowy-global-network-right-wing-think-tanks) from all over the world, but particularly the US.
4. It’s based on an [unscientific and outdated approach to economics](https://www.theguardian.com/news/2017/jul/11/how-economics-became-a-religion) that disregards evidence in favor of a priori reasoning and unfalsifiable models.
5. Key parts of its agenda have been [shown by modern evidence-based research to deliver little growth while increasing inequality](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2016/06/ostry.htm).

To be clear: this is not to say there is absolutely nothing to salvage from neoliberal ideology. The era of trade liberalization saw [hundreds of millions of people lifted out of extreme poverty](https://ourworldindata.org/data-insights/was-the-global-decline-of-extreme-poverty-only-due-to-china), stabilization plans like Brazil’s Plano Real [ended the hyperinflation that was destroying workers’ purchasing power](https://library.brown.edu/create/fivecenturiesofchange/chapters/chapter-8/cardoso/), and the opening of telecom markets enabled innovations like [mobile banking, which has measurably reduced poverty in Kenya](https://news.mit.edu/2016/mobile-money-kenyans-out-poverty-1208).

But that's the problem with Neoliberalism: it bundles together legitimate, empirically validated policy (which gives it legitimacy in the eyes of the public) with illegitimate, empirically debunked policy that harms working people and only benefits oligarchs.

In my experience, when confronted with these facts, libertarians usually give up consequentialism and start arguing from a deontological perspective that defines right and wrong on the basis of first principles such as "taxation is theft" or "natural rights to property". But these "first principles" are mere pseudoaxioms, resting on nothing but the subjective intuition of its proponents, far from being as universal as the utilitarian intuition we all share regarding the badness of suffering. That's why neoliberalism can't stand as a rational, evidence-based, testable ideology. It is always either pseudoscientific or deontological.

## A scientific and consequentialist ideology

I believe the first truth we must acknowledge when discussing scientific, consequentialist ideologies is that there are potentially many of them. As Sam Harris argues, "there are many peaks in the moral landscape". There are potentially infinitely many systems that could deliver the same amount of well being and freedom from suffering. As somebody with little formal education in the fields of political science and philosophy, I adopt a pragmatic approach and try to promote systems that already exist in the literature rather than proposing entirely new ones from scratch. After a lifetime of learning about these systems in school, university, books, and Wikipedia rabbit holes, I was inspired by the history of revolutionary anarchism and today I tentatively identify as structural reformist and a supporter of economic democracy.

### Revolutionary social anarchism

I have always been frustrated by the false dichotomy between "capitalism" and "socialism", and so I have naturally searched for innovative alternatives. In the process of reading about the history of these ideologies, however, I learned that the ideological warfare that defined the revolutionary period of the 20th century was far from binary. The anti-capitalist revolutionaries were far from unified, and the centralized form of state socialism with top-down planned economies that ultimately became the norm in the so-called "Second World" was only one of multiple ideologies fighting for hegemony in the battle against the capitalist oppression of workers. The main opponents of state-led socialism were [the anarchists](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/anarchism/). This rivalry goes back to the [conflict between Karl Marx and Mikhail Bakunin](https://libcom.org/article/first-socialist-schism-bakunin-vs-marx-international-working-mens-association-wolfgang) in the First International, which ended with the expulsion of the anarchists in 1872. The movement kept splintering after that, giving rise to three more Internationals: the social-democratic Second, the Soviet-led Third, and the Trotskyist Fourth.

It is shocking, if not downright suspicious, how little we learn about this in the West. From school age we are indoctrinated with this false dichotomy between free, decentralized markets and centralized, planned economies led by authoritarian states. "Anarchism" became [synonymous with "chaos and disorder"](https://theconversation.com/what-is-anarchism-all-about-50373), erasing an entire history of anarchist philosophy. If taken seriously at all, anarchism is often associated with right-wing libertarians, anarcho-capitalists, crypto-anarchists, etc. But these radically individualistic versions of anarchism are [extremely recent, and a particularly American phenomenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarcho-capitalism). In the history of anarchist thought, [most authors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarchism) have proposed some form of [social anarchism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_anarchism) that emphasizes not only the autonomy of individuals within communities, but the autonomy of communities within larger social structures. So what's the version of anarchism that I support and why?

Anarchism is, at its core, a radically anti-authoritarian ideology. It questions any hierarchy in society and opposes any unequal distribution of power. People often think of anarchism as being "anti-state", and it is true that many anarchists oppose the nation-state, but it would be unwise to mistake this position for a defining principle of anarchism. It is a position that many *derive* from anarchist principles, but it is not *a foundational principle in itself*. I used to dismiss anarchism as naive and utopian because I couldn't imagine a revolution replacing the nation-state with an anarchist society overnight. But after reading Chomsky, Graeber, Kropotkin, and others, I realized I was the one naively misinterpreting the definition of anarchism.

> The core of the anarchist tradition, as I understand it, is that power is always illegitimate, unless it proves itself to be legitimate. So the burden of proof is always on those who claim that some authoritarian hierarchic relation is legitimate. If they can’t prove it, then it should be dismantled.
>
> — Noam Chomsky, [interview with Harry Kreisler](https://chomsky.info/20020322/), 2002

The most common form of anarchism defended by real movements in recent history is often called ["anarcho-syndicalism"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarcho-syndicalism). It is basically a system where companies are not nationalized and taken over by a state bureaucracy, but instead they are taken over directly by the workers themselves. This is not only a theoretical system, it [actually existed briefly during the Spanish civil war](https://www.vscw.ca/en/node/16), when factory workers effectively overthrew their capitalist overlords and took the means of production, transforming for-profit, authoritarian companies into democratically-controlled, worker-owned cooperatives. It was also [attempted in socialist Yugoslavia from 1950 onward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_self-management), and is still practiced today in places like [Rojava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_Administration_of_North_and_East_Syria) and to some extent [Chiapas, Mexico](https://www.opendemocracy.net/en/democraciaabierta/zapatistas-lecciones-de-auto-organizaci%C3%B3n-comunitaria-en/).

And does it work? You might ask. Unfortunately, concrete examples of actually functioning anarcho-syndicalist regimes are hard to find, and when they are found it is usually in unstable regions, where authoritarian forces (capitalist, communist, or theocratic), haven't been able to crush it yet. This makes the efficacy of these regimes hard to measure, which brings us to the next topic.

### Structural reform and economic democracy

Anarchism is often discussed in the context of revolutions. Indeed, the most vocal supporters of anarchism in recent history were the anti-capitalist revolutionaries of the 19th and 20th centuries, and they actually succeeded briefly in Eastern Spain, before they were crushed by Franco. However, revolution is not the only way to create an anarchist society. Just like the Scandinavians managed to implement a [somewhat egalitarian form of social democracy via gradual reforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordic_model), there's nothing stopping us from gradually building an anarchist society in a similar fashion. That would be much more plausible than a revolution anywhere in the Americas, Europe, or basically any country that's not unstable enough for a civil war or revolution to be realistic in the near future. So how can we achieve this? And why should we want to?

In spite of being deeply misunderstood, the principles guiding social anarchists are not as exotic as one might think. Indeed, worker-owned cooperatives exist in many countries, [even hyper-capitalist ones like the US](https://www.usworker.coop/census/). In Spain, [Mondragon](https://www.thenews.coop/mondragon-posts-positive-2025-revenue-and-job-figures/) employs over 71,000 people and generated over €600 million in profits in 2025. Some countries even have legislation actively encouraging employee ownership. In the US, [Employee Stock Ownership Plans (ESOPs)](https://www.nceo.org/research/employee-ownership-by-the-numbers) cover more than 15 million workers and hold over $2 trillion in assets. Since 1985, Italy's [Marcora Law](https://ica.coop/en/media/news/marcora-law-supporting-worker-buyouts-thirty-years) has allowed laid-off workers to invest their unemployment benefits into buying out their failing companies and running them as cooperatives. In France, since 2014, [owners of small companies must inform their employees before selling](https://cecop.coop/works/heading-towards-a-cooperative-shockwave-the-adoption-of-the-law-on-the-social-and-solidarity-economy-in-france), so that they have a chance to submit a bid of their own. And in the UK, owners who sell a controlling stake to an [Employee Ownership Trust](https://www.shoosmiths.com/insights/articles/employee-ownership-trusts) pay no capital gains tax — a model inspired by the [John Lewis Partnership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lewis_Partnership), a retailer owned by its 74,000 employees since 1929.

From the implementation of the New Deal in the 1930s until Reagan's presidency in the 80s, the US and much of the Western World experienced the Golden Age of welfare capitalism. There was a true sense of optimism, belief in the system, trust in institutions, and social mobility was a realistic ambition rather than a utopian fantasy. The ruling class had learned a lesson that is still echoed in the speech of contemporary politicians:

> You don’t stop socialism by throwing slogans at people about the free market […]. As much as I love the free market, you stop socialism by making people’s lives better, and that’s what we’re trying to do every single day.
>
> — JD Vance, [New York Post](https://nypost.com/2026/08/14/us-news/jd-vance-warns-republicans-not-to-blame-young-americans-for-the-appeal-of-socialism/), 2026

The center-left and the center-right managed to find a compromise. The welfare state was expanded and the lives of white working people were improved just enough to appease the left and undermine the momentum of more radical movements that demanded structural reform. The oppression of the black population in the US was still severe enough to radicalize many into joining movements such at the Black Panthers, which was crushed by the FBI with the infamous assassinations of Fred Hampton and Mark Clark. But still, the successes of the civil rights movement were enough to give people some degree of hope that the system works and change is possible. As we reached the "end of history", with Western liberal democracy becaming the dominant political system around the world after the fall of the Soviet Union, terms like "anarchism", "anarcho-syndicalism", or even "libertarian socialism" became anachronistic. The specter of Communism was no longer haunting Europe, and revolution was no longer in the air.

Today, [economic democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Economic_democracy&oldid=1345662943) is the terminology commonly used by those who propose to "shift ownership and decision-making power from corporate shareholders and corporate managers (such as a board of directors) to a larger group of public stakeholders that includes workers, consumers, suppliers, communities and the broader public". Indeed, the Marcora Law and ESOPs mentioned earlier are examples of institutions that came into being via gradual, structural reform — and Mondragon shows the same can be achieved from below, having grown from [a single workshop founded in 1956](https://www.mondragon-corporation.com/en/history/) into a federation of cooperatives without any revolution, or even a sympathetic government.

In circles that embrace radical, revolutionary language, this terminology may be frowned upon. But again, overindulging in these linguistic debates over essentially contested concepts risks distracting us from doing the work necessary to actually achieve any concrete victories. That's why I try to interpret people charitably, regardless of the language they use, and support the adoption of different strategies at the same time. If you live in an unstable country with a strong anarchist movement that could realistically overthrow the regime, then a revolutionary strategy might make sense. If you live in a country with a strong democracy and leaders who are potentially sympathetic to economic democracy, traditional activism and public pressure might be sufficient. In other contexts, a DIY approach that focuses on creating dual power structures from the ground up might be the best way to go. Most countries don't fit any of these categories perfectly, so we'd probably benefit from taking inspiration from all strategies.

### Compatibility with scientific consequentialism

In the last few paragraphs, I have attempted to provide a brief description and history of the political/economic philosophy I have come to tentatively support. But why is this philosophy more compatible with scientific consequentialism than any others? That's what I will explain in the following sections.

#### Fallibilism

[Fallibilism](https://iep.utm.edu/fallibil/) is a philosophical position that acknowledges the fact that we can almost never know anything for sure. According to this view, all we can ever have are tentative hypotheses that we are only justified in holding with partial degrees of confidence. As I have argued before, this is a core value of a scientific epistemology. As [Popper and others have argued](https://iep.utm.edu/popp-pol/#:~:text=Democracy%20happens%20to%20be%20the,get%20rid%20of%20bad%20rulers), the beauty of democracy is exactly its role as a self-improving mechanism within a political context. What's the best possible political/economic system? Under a consequentialist framework, this is an empirical question that we will never have a final answer to. That's why whatever system we create needs to be easily updatable.

Economic democracy, therefore, should be even more compatible with the scientific ethos than the economic authoritarianism under which we live today. If democracy serves a positive role as a self-correction mechanism in the political realm, why wouldn't it be equally beneficial in the economic realm? In fact, that's how [many define anarchism](https://reason.com/2025/06/10/the-best-democracy-is-anarchy/): radically embracing democracy in all realms of life. Anarchists support a much more expanded, participatory, grassroots, decentralized democracy in the political realm, democratic deliberation in the workplace, democratically run schools in the educational realm, etc.

In radical leftist circles, many express skepticism towards Karl Popper, often accusing him of being a "liberal", which is another essentially contested term that people use very differently depending on their ideological bent. As I have mentioned earlier, part of this skepticism is directed towards the reformism implied by his views, which I have already responded to, but another part might also be related to his criticism of Karl Marx. Radical leftists tend to use the word "liberal" to describe moderates who support socially progressive welfare capitalism instead of structural reform and worker-centered politics. But again, none of that follows from Popperian philosophy.

Popper's criticism of Marx was aimed primarily at his method of [historicist prophecy and wholesale utopian engineering](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/#:~:text=Historicism%20he%20identified%20as%20the,rules%20towards%20a%20determinate%20end&text=large%20scale%20social%20planning%20to%20an%20antecedently%20conceived%20blueprint%20is%20inherently%20misconceived), not at his egalitarian ends. In [*Unended Quest*](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/420091-i-remained-a-socialist-for-several-years-even-after-my) he describes remaining a socialist for several years after abandoning Marxism: "[I]f there could be such a thing as socialism combined with individual liberty, I would be a socialist still. For nothing could be better than living a modest, simple, and free life in an egalitarian society" — though he eventually dismissed this as "no more than a beautiful dream", on the grounds that "the attempt to realize equality endangers freedom". [*The Open Society*](http://www.the-rathouse.com/OpenSocietyOnLIne/Chapter-20-Capitalism-and-its-Fate.html#:~:text=how%20justified%20he%20was%20in,the%20hell%20of%20unrestrained%20capitalism) itself explicitly grants that Marx's account of unrestrained capitalism's injustice was substantially right for his time. Popper's ["paradox of freedom" argument](http://www.the-rathouse.com/OpenSocietyOnLIne/Chapter17-Legal-Social-System.html#:~:text=We%20must%20demand%20that%20unrestrained%20capitalism%20give%20way%20to%20an%20economic%20interventionism) — unlimited economic freedom lets the economically strong coerce the weak, so the state must intervene — is an argument for economic interventionism, and he says plainly that laissez-faire capitalism must be replaced by what he calls economic interventionism.

It may be true that in Popperian/rationalist circles there are many people that identify as liberals and who support welfare capitalism and reject labels such as socialism and communism, but these positions don't follow necessarily from the Popperian arguments I am relying on. Making that assumption would be falling prey to the fallacy of guilt by association. On the contrary, as I have argued in this section, it is by no means a stretch to derive anarchist conclusions from his philosophy. As an evidence-based consequentialist, however, I don't think any political system can be properly defended a priori from the armchair. So what empirical data do we have to justify our confidence in the belief that economic democracy would be good for the world?
